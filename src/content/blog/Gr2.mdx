### By Lyfong Chuon, Sreypich Norn, and Kongpheng Tang

Navigating Phnom Penh’s congested intersections is itself a test of patience, but for many commuters, the journey comes with added hazards: floating ash and secondhand smoke.

Across urban areas in the country, motorcyclists flicking cigarette ash into traffic or ride-hailing riders lighting up behind the wheel are a common sight, and a problem. Experts and passengers warned that smoking while driving falls into a “dangerous gray zone” between public health policy and road safety enforcement.

For drivers on the road for long hours, smoking is viewed as a “necessity” rather than a distraction.

“I’ve been smoking for 10 years. It is not about relieving stress anymore. Smoking is a form of emotional support for me,” said a 24-year-old, who identified herself as Ell.

She frequently smokes and vapes while on the road. Despite two previous attempts to quit, stress continues to pull her back into the habit.

While the 2015 Law on Tobacco Control protects non-smokers from secondhand smoke, a loophole in the law leaves private motorbike passengers unprotected on the road.

According to Sub-Decree No. 43 ANK.BK issued by the Health Ministry in 2016, smoking is banned in workplaces, health facilities and public transportation, including taxis, buses, and passenger boats. Those who violate the regulation could face a fine of 20,000 riel (approximately $5).

However, the sub-decree does not apply to private vehicles and motorbikes. Similarly, the 2007 Law on Land Traffic does not explicitly list smoking while on a motorcycle as a distinct driving offence. The gap creates uncertainty for commuters, where public transit riders are protected by smoke-free rules, but passengers on private motorbikes are exposed to secondhand smoke and flying ash.

However, non-smoking passengers and nearby motorcyclists often experience the effects and frustration, having to share the road with smokers.

Bun Leap, a 20-year-old university student who frequently encounters drivers who smoke on the road, expressed concern over the practice.

“I get really annoyed by smokers on the road,” he said, adding that people who light up while riding their bikes show a lack of consideration for others. “Secondhand smoke seriously affects people nearby, especially pregnant women.”
Lika, 24, a vendor at Orussey Market in the capital, said she often sees motorists smoking behind the wheel while navigating traffic.

“I dislike the smell of cigarette smoke because it makes me cough,” she remarked, adding that she usually speeds up or steers her vehicle away from a smoker in traffic. Lika urged the authorities to issue a warning to motorists who smoke on the road.

While some riders view smoking as a personal habit, health experts said everyone around the smoker is affected by the smoke.

“The smoke does not remain with the smoker,” said a technical officer at World Health Organisation (WHO) Cambodia. “Secondhand smoke is not merely an unpleasant smell. It’s a mixture of smoke from the burning end of a cigarette and smoke exhaled by the smoker.”

He said secondhand smoke is a serious health hazard for people who do not smoke. “It contains a toxic mixture of more than 7,000 chemicals. The exposure can cause serious health problems in non-smokers, including heart disease, stroke and lung cancer,” he added.

The WHO technical officer explained that smoking in vehicles creates a particular risk because smoke can build up quickly in a small space.

“The risk is particularly significant inside vehicles, as they are small, enclosed spaces where tobacco smoke can quickly accumulate to high concentrations,” the officer said, adding that passengers might not be able to open a window.

For motorbike passengers, the exposure is lower as they are not in an enclosed environment like in a car, but it is still a direct risk. The same applies to a ride-hailing passenger who sits behind the rider.

“Opening windows and ventilation does not eliminate the risk. The only effective way to protect passengers is to keep the vehicle completely smoke-free,” the officer said.

Children need more protection because they cannot easily move away from smoke, he noted. “They are particularly vulnerable because their lungs and immune systems are still developing, and cannot easily remove themselves from the source of smoke,” the officer said.

Addressing smoking behind the wheel in Cambodia involves navigating the boundary between public health mandates and transport policy.

While Sub-Decree No. 43 establishes smoke-free environments in passenger transport to protect them from secondhand smoke, general road regulations primarily focus on active driving offences.

Health advocates and policy observers note that clearer public guidance on “distracted driving”, alongside education on secondhand smoke exposure in vehicles could help close the gap between health regulations and daily commuting habits.
